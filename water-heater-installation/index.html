Water heater installation in Randolph, NJ covers choosing the right type of unit, running its water, fuel or electrical connections, venting it where required, and getting the township permit sorted. It applies to a first install in a new space, a switch from one fuel to another, or an upgrade you plan ahead of any failure.

Planning ahead gives you room to compare options calmly. Call us and we talk through the house, the fuel you already have and what the township office will want to see.

Pick a type

Gas, electric or heat pump tanks

The fuel you can use usually decides the shortlist. Randolph homes run on natural gas, oil, propane or electricity, and New Jersey Natural Gas has been installing gas main in parts of town, though it does not serve every address. Ask us to check what your house has before picking a model.

Gas and vent

Venting and gas lines are not the place to cut corners

A gas or oil heater has to carry exhaust out of the house. A vent that slopes the wrong way, has gaps, or is blocked can push combustion gases into the home. Because carbon monoxide has no smell, New Jersey law requires CO alarms in existing covered structures unless there is no CO hazard, and a new gas unit is a good time to test yours.

Suspect a gas leak at any point and leave first. Move well away from the house, then call the gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not flip switches or start a car nearby.

Paperwork

Permits, plumbers and who can do the work

State rules say plumbing is done by a master plumber, an authorized plumbing contractor, or employees working under a master plumber's supervision. A single-family homeowner may work on his or her own dwelling. In Randolph, a water heater needs a construction permit through the Office of Construction Codes, and no zoning approval is involved.

The plumbing subcode form is signed and sealed by a licensed plumber unless an owner-occupant is doing the job. Because the township asks people to call during planning, scheduling a visit early helps. You can also check any plumber's license through the state Division of Consumer Affairs verification system.

Switching

Moving from a gas tank to a heat pump unit

A gas-to-heat-pump change is mostly an electrical and space question. The new unit needs a circuit, a spot with enough air around it, and a place to send condensate. The old gas line is capped and the vent is removed or sealed by a licensed professional.

If the house has little room, a standard electric tank or a gas replacement may fit better. We lay out what works in your space and let you pick.

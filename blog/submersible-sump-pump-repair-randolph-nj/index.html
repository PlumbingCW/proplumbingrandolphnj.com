Submersible Sump Pump Repair: What to Fix and When to Replace

A plain guide to stuck floats, nonstop running, check valve trouble and the repair-or-replace decision for Randolph, NJ basements.

Most submersible sump pump repairs come down to three parts: the float switch, the check valve and the pump motor itself. A pump that will not start usually has a stuck or tangled float, a pump that never stops often has the same float or a failed check valve, and a pump that hums without moving water may have a jammed impeller. The pit and the discharge line matter just as much as the pump.

If you are in Randolph, NJ and standing in a basement with water rising, the first move is to unplug the pump, check that the float moves freely, and call for help if it does not. The sections below explain what each symptom means, what a repair involves, and how to weigh repair against replacement.

The switch

Float switch problems: the most common failure

The float switch tells the pump when to start and stop. It rises with the water, triggers the motor, and drops when the pit empties. If the float wedges against the pit wall, catches on the power cord or sits in sludge, the pump either never starts or never stops.

Lift the float by hand with the pump plugged in. If it kicks on, the switch works and the float was caught. If nothing happens, the switch may have failed or the pump may be dead. A tethered float is vulnerable to tangling, and a vertical float switch is more tolerant of a narrow pit.

Replacing a switch is a modest repair on a pump that is otherwise healthy. On an older pump, it is worth asking whether the motor has years left before paying to extend its life.

Nonstop

Why a sump pump runs nonstop

A pump that never shuts off has one of three problems. The float may be jammed in the up position, so the pump thinks the pit is full. The check valve may have failed, letting water from the discharge pipe fall back into the pit so the pump pushes the same water again and again. Or water is entering the pit faster than the pump can clear it.

Listen when the pump stops. A loud thump or a rush of water returning to the pit points to the check valve. If the pit is full and the water level is not dropping, the pump may be undersized or the discharge line may be blocked, frozen or kinked.

A pump that runs all the time wears itself out quickly. Treat nonstop running as a repair to do soon, not something to monitor for weeks.

One-way flow

The check valve and why it sticks or fails

A check valve sits on the discharge pipe just above the pump and lets water leave but not come back. Over time the flapper or ball inside wears, and grit can keep it from sealing. A noisy valve with a hard slam on shutoff is a sign it is working hard, and a valve that leaks back will make the pump cycle constantly.

Replacing a check valve is quick compared with replacing a pump. It is also a place where old, glued fittings can be hard to remove, so we cut and rebuild the section with unions that allow service later. A pit and a floor drain are separate systems, so if a basement floor drain also backs up, our drain cleaning team clears that line on its own.

Cost logic

Repair or replace: how we decide

Repair makes sense when the motor is healthy and the failed part is a float, a check valve, a cord or a clogged intake. Replace when the housing is cracked, the motor is burned out, the bearings are noisy, or the pump has failed more than once in a short time. A new pump also gives you a chance to correct the pit and the discharge routing.

Age is a clue but not a rule. Pumps in busy pits work harder and wear sooner than pumps that run a few times a year. We look at how often it cycles, what the pit contains, and whether the pump is the right size for the water it handles.

  • ✓Choose repair when only one inexpensive part has failed
  • ✓Choose replacement when the motor, housing or bearings are the problem
  • ✓Add a battery backup whenever the pit protects finished space or stored belongings
  • ✓Keep a second small pump or backup if the pit sees frequent heavy inflow
Prevention

Maintenance you can do in ten minutes

  1. 1

    Look in the pit

    Remove the lid, clear debris, and confirm the pump sits upright and not on gravel or silt.

  2. 2

    Test the float

    Pour in water until the float rises and the pump starts, then watch it shut off cleanly.

  3. 3

    Check the discharge

    Confirm the outside end of the pipe is clear and drains away from the foundation.

  4. 4

    Test the backup

    If you have a battery unit, check the charge indicator and test it on schedule.

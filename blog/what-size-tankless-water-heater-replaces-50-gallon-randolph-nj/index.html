What Size Tankless Water Heater Replaces a 50 Gallon Tank?

There is no direct swap. Tankless units are sized by flow and temperature rise, not by gallons.

No tankless water heater is the equivalent of a 50 gallon tank, because the two are measured in different units. A tank is sized by how much hot water it stores. A tankless unit is rated by the temperature rise it can deliver at a given flow rate, in gallons per minute. To replace a 50 gallon tank in Randolph, NJ, you size the new unit to the most hot water you use at one time.

The method below follows the Department of Energy approach. It takes about ten minutes with a pencil, and it keeps you from buying a unit that runs out of hot water on a busy morning.

Why gallons do not convert to GPM

A 50 gallon tank holds a reserve and recovers slowly. A tankless unit holds almost nothing and heats water as it flows. The reserve is irrelevant to the new unit, so the old tank size tells you very little.

What matters is how many fixtures run together and how cold your incoming water is. Two showers and a dishwasher at once is a very different load from a single bathroom sink.

The sizing method in four steps

  1. 1

    List the fixtures used at the same time

    Think about your busiest hour. Which taps, showers, appliances would run at once?

  2. 2

    Add up their flow rates

    Each fixture has a flow rating in GPM. Add the ones that overlap.

  3. 3

    Work out the temperature rise

    Subtract the incoming water temperature from the temperature you want out of the tap.

  4. 4

    Match a unit rated for that flow at that rise

    Check the maker's chart, which lists GPM available at each rise. Pick a unit that meets or beats your number.

A worked example with real numbers

Say the household runs a bathroom faucet at 0.75 GPM while someone showers at 2.5 GPM. That is 0.75 plus 2.5, or 3.25 GPM. The DOE guide assumes water comes in at 50 degrees F and leaves at 120 degrees F unless you know otherwise, so the rise is 70 degrees F.

A gas unit typically delivers about 5 GPM at a 70 degree rise, so it covers the 3.25 GPM load with room to spare. An electric unit typically delivers about 2 GPM at that rise, so it falls short of the shower alone.

Now add a second shower at 2.5 GPM. The total becomes 5.75 GPM, which is more than a typical 5 GPM gas unit can deliver at a 70 degree rise. That household needs a larger gas unit, or a plan to avoid running both showers together.

Gas line and vent checks before you buy

  • ✓Is the existing gas pipe large enough to feed a tankless burner, which draws more fuel at once than a tank burner?
  • ✓Does the vent need to change to a different material or a new route?
  • ✓Is there a condensate drain nearby, if the unit is a condensing model?
  • ✓Is there an electrical outlet nearby for the unit's controls?
  • ✓Is the water supply pressure and pipe size adequate for the rated flow?

If you smell gas at any point, leave the building first, then call the gas utility or 911 from outside.

Common sizing mistakes

Sizing by tank gallons

A 50 gallon label tells you nothing about flow. Use GPM and rise.

Ignoring cold incoming water

A colder supply means a higher rise, which lowers the flow a unit can deliver.

Forgetting overlap

The dishwasher and laundry may run while someone showers. Count them.

Skipping the gas line

A great unit with an undersized pipe will cut out under load.

A sewer inspection in Randolph, NJ sends a waterproof camera through your main drain line so a licensed plumber can see its real condition. The camera records roots, cracks, offsets, sags and buildup, and a locator marks where each one sits above ground.

This is a planned visit, not an emergency call. It suits home buyers, owners with repeat clogs and anyone about to put money into a yard, driveway or addition. If sewage is coming up now, the 24/7 line also reaches us, but this page is about looking before something fails.

Buying a house in Randolph? Look at the sewer before closing

A home inspection normally does not follow the main drain out to the street. A camera does. Putting one through the line during the inspection period shows whether the seller is handing you a root-filled or cracked pipe, and the footage gives you something concrete to negotiate with.

If the house is on a private well, the sale involves a separate step. New Jersey requires well water to be tested at sale under the Private Well Testing Act, and a certified lab does that. Plumbers do not. Ask your agent to schedule it alongside the sewer camera so closing is not delayed by either.

What the camera finds

  • ✓Roots entering at joints, and how thick they are
  • ✓Cracks, breaks and missing pipe sections
  • ✓Bellies, where the pipe has sagged and holds water
  • ✓Offsets, where joints sit out of line
  • ✓Scale and grease coating the wall
  • ✓Connections, including where a second line joins

We note the pipe material when it is visible. That is useful because a house's age does not tell you what is under it. Randolph's housing is mixed, with 54 percent built before 1980, so each line has to be looked at on its own.

How a camera inspection works, step by step

  1. 1

    Pick the entry point

    The plumber uses a cleanout if there is one. Otherwise a toilet is pulled or a roof vent is opened.

  2. 2

    Run the camera

    A flexible rod feeds the camera along the line while it records.

  3. 3

    Locate faults

    A receiver above ground picks up the camera's signal and marks depth and position.

  4. 4

    Review together

    You see the footage, and the plumber explains what matters and what does not.

Sewer or septic: what the inspection covers

Randolph's public sewer has 82 miles of main and 10 pumping stations, but the township owns no treatment plant. Where homes sit on septic instead, the camera still looks at the line from the house to the tank. It does not inspect the tank, and the tank needs its own check.

We cannot see into the public main past your property line. If the footage suggests the trouble is beyond it, we tell you and you can bring that evidence to the township's Division of Water and Sewer.

Not every finding means digging. A small coating can be cleaned, and a minor root problem can be monitored. The point of the inspection is to separate what needs money now from what can wait.

Permits and repairs that follow an inspection

Inspecting is not construction, but repair might be. New Jersey requires a construction permit for work on existing buildings, though ordinary maintenance including routine repair is exempt. The local enforcing agency decides where a given job falls.

If the camera leads to piping work, ask the Randolph Office of Construction Codes before work starts. If it only calls for clearing a clog, a drain cleaning visit may cover it.
